Meditate

This project was developed as the Senior Project for the Computer Science major at Yale University. The goal of the project was to explore ways of cultural preservation towards a more interactive, personalized method, going beyond recreating works with computer graphics. At the final presentation, visitors wore real EEG headsets, and their brainwaves were recorded to create personalized artworks in the form of dancheong, the painted decoration found on Korean Buddhist temples.

Try the installation from your browser. You will first sit still for a short baseline recording, and then you will have 60 seconds to find focus and meditation. As you focus, the screen will outline floral motifs found in Korean Buddhist art to show your level of intention. At the end, you will receive a brainwave chart in the form of Korean Buddhist art, where your signals are matched to specific visual motifs.

You can use a simulated signal, or connect your own Muse headset in Chrome or Edge. Find a quiet place before you begin.

Reading your chart

Read your chart from left to right. The diamonds on the inner strips mark every twenty seconds, and the panel is mirrored from top to bottom to stay consistent with traditional dancheong. The legend below shows how your brain activity was mapped to dancheong motifs.

Ribbons and small blossoms fill the space between, colored by whichever band led at that moment. The focus score compares beta with alpha and theta, scaled to your own recording, so treat it as a rough signal from a consumer headset. Motif names follow common dancheong usage, which differs between lineages of artisans.

A personal touch

Dancheong has a strict color system, with an internal grammar that restorers retain in cultural preservation and art creation. This chart is my own artistic reading and reconstruction of the tradition, personalized in my style while retaining the motifs, grammar, and rulesets as closely as possible. I made it as my interpretation of dancheong, so please don't take it for a restoration.

Dancheong and Korean Buddhist art

Dancheong is written with the characters for red and blue. It is the painted decoration on Korea's wooden temples and palaces, where it protects timber from decay and insects and marks a building's authority. In principle, only palaces and Buddhist temples were allowed it. Its colors follow the five elements and their directions, with blue for east, white for west, red for south, black for north and yellow for the center.

Its patterns fall into four groups, sorted by where they are painted. Meoricho sits at the ends of beams, byeoljihwa are pictures painted in the space between them, geummun brocade patterns fill the middle of members with treasures and geometry, and ceiling patterns give each panel a single flower, animal or shape. With the rich cultural context and history, I treated dancheong as a grammar and explored how it could be created with code.

Its history

The oldest examples appear in Goguryeo tomb murals. In Anak Tomb No. 3, built in 357, the painted beams carry cloud patterns and the ceiling holds a red lotus. The saw-tooth beam ends in those murals are the earliest form of meoricho, and the pattern can be traced into the Goryeo period, whose dancheong survives at the Geungnakjeon of Bongjeongsa and the Daeungjeon of Sudeoksa.

Under the Joseon dynasty, Neo-Confucianism became the state ideology and many temple properties were confiscated. In 1424, the court ordered that only 36 temples be kept. During the Japanese invasions of 1592 to 1598, treasures were plundered from Buddhist monasteries, and most temple buildings older than the sixteenth century were destroyed. The statues found in most temples today were made in the late Joseon period, so much of the work of preservation is relearning how this art functioned.

Preserving it today

Repainting
Dancheong has to be repainted, either the whole building or only its damaged parts, and each repainting is a decision about what the original looked like.
Lost methods
Sungnyemun, Seoul's great south gate, burned in 2008 and reopened in May 2013 after a restoration of about 27.7 billion won. Within months, inspectors found at least 100 major cases of cracking and flaking in the new dancheong. Experts pointed to the handling of a traditional glue, and one professor questioned the pigments imported from Japan.
Living knowledge
Dancheong masters are recognized as National Intangible Cultural Heritage. In one study, six apprentices of masters prepared palettes totaling 20 colors, and only 12 appeared in all six. The authors note that the final colors at national heritage sites have no available records.
Scattered works
Paintings of the Ten Kings of Hell were taken from Sinheungsa around the end of the Korean War. LACMA returned six of them in 2020 and the Metropolitan Museum returned The Tenth King of Hell in November 2025. Seven of the ten are back, and no one knows where the other three are.
